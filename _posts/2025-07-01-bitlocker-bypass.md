This post outlines the methodology for sniffing BitLocker Volume Master Keys
(VMK) from a Trusted Platform Module (TPM) on a target system. This technique is
effective against default BitLocker configurations that do not use pre-boot
authentication like a PIN or startup key.

## Introduction and Background

BitLocker is a full-volume encryption feature included with Microsoft Windows.
In its default configuration, it uses a TPM to store and release the encryption
keys automatically at boot. This provides a seamless user experience but can
expose the encryption key to physical attacks.

The TPM is a dedicated microcontroller designed to secure hardware through
integrated cryptographic keys. However, the communication between the CPU and a
discrete TPM (dTPM) chip on the motherboard often occurs over an unencrypted
bus, typically SPI or LPC. By sniffing this communication during the boot
process, it is possible to capture the VMK as it's sent from the TPM to the CPU.
This key can then be used to decrypt the entire contents of the
BitLocker-encrypted drive.

**Note:** This attack is only practical against discrete TPMs (dTPMs).
Firmware-based TPMs (fTPMs), which are integrated into the CPU, are generally
not vulnerable to this specific bus-sniffing technique (unless you are Ant-Man).

## Understanding the Communication Bus Signals

To successfully intercept the communication, it is crucial to understand the
role of each pin on the target bus. The two most common buses for discrete TPMs
are SPI and LPC.

### SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) Bus

SPI is a synchronous serial communication interface used for short-distance
communication, primarily in embedded systems. It operates in a main-secondary
architecture where the main (CPU/Super I/O) directs communication with one or
more secondaries (the TPM). The essential signals are:

  * **MOSI (Main Out, Secondary In):** This is the data line that carries
    information *from* the main device (the CPU) *to* the secondary device (the
    TPM). When sniffing, this line will show you the commands being sent to the
    TPM, such as a request for the BitLocker key.
  * **MISO (Main In, Secondary Out):** This is the data line that carries
    information *from* the secondary device (the TPM) *to* the main (the CPU).
    **This is the most critical signal for this attack**, as it is the line on
    which the TPM will transmit the BitLocker Volume Master Key back to the CPU
    after a valid request.
  * **SCLK (Serial Clock):** This clock signal, generated by the main,
    synchronizes the data transfer between the main and secondary. All data on
    the MOSI and MISO lines is timed according to the rising or falling edge of
    this clock.
  * **CS (Chip Select):** Also known as Secondary Select (SS), this signal is
    used by the main to select which specific secondary device it wishes to
    communicate with. When this line is pulled low (it is typically active-low),
    the corresponding secondary chip is activated. This is essential for
    isolating the communication intended for the TPM from other noise on the
    bus.

### LPC (Low Pin Count) Bus

The LPC bus is a parallel bus used in PCs to connect low-bandwidth devices (like
Super I/O controllers, BIOS ROM, and dTPMs) to the CPU. It was designed as a
successor to the ISA bus but uses fewer pins.

  * **LAD\[0:3\] (LPC Address/Data):** These are four bidirectional lines that
    are multiplexed, meaning they carry both address and data information at
    different times within a transaction. To reconstruct the full communication,
    you must monitor all four of these lines simultaneously. Both the commands
    to the TPM and the key sent back from it will travel over these lines.
  * **LFRAME# (LPC Frame):** This is the primary control signal on the LPC bus
    and is active-low (indicated by the `#` symbol). When the main pulls this
    line low, it signals the beginning of a new transaction cycle, or "frame,"
    on the bus. This signal is the equivalent of a "start" button for a bus
    operation and is essential for the logic analyzer to know when to begin
    interpreting the data on the LAD lines.
  * **LCLK (LPC Clock):** This is the main system clock for the LPC bus,
    typically running at a frequency of 33 MHz. It synchronizes all data
    transfers across the LAD lines, ensuring that all devices on the bus can
    correctly interpret the signals.

## Hardware and Software Requirements

To successfully perform this attack, you will need specific hardware and
software tools.

### Hardware

  * **Logic Analyzer**:

      * **Recommended**: [Saleae
        Logic 8](https://www.saleae.com/products/saleae-logic-8). This is a
        versatile and user-friendly logic analyzer that is well-suited for this
        type of work.
      * **Alternative**: Any logic analyzer that is compatible with the Saleae
        Logic 2 software will also work. Ensure the analyzer supports a
        sufficient sample rate (at least 4x the bus speed, so \~100MHz is a safe
        bet).

  * **Connection Tools**: The method for connecting to the TPM will vary
    depending on the target system's motherboard layout. Some options (in order
    from easiest to most difficult)

      * **SOIC8 Clip**: If the TPM is in a standard SOIC8 package, a SOIC8 test
        clip can be used to connect to the pins without soldering. This is a
        less invasive and quicker method. ![SOIC8
        Clip](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/soic8_clip.jpg)
      * **PCBite Probes**: For accessing small test points, vias on the
        motherboard, or making gravity-assisted contact with the TPM pins,
        PCBite probes are an excellent choice. They are high-precision, steady
        probes that can be positioned accurately without the need for soldering.
        ![PCBite Probes](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/pcbite_probes.jpg)
      * **Soldering Iron and Fine-gauge Wire**: In many cases, the most reliable
        method is to solder wires directly to the TPM chip's pins or to exposed
        vias on the motherboard. This requires a good quality,
        temperature-controlled soldering iron, fine-gauge wire (e.g., 30 AWG),
        and soldering flux.

  * **Screwdrivers and Prying Tools**: A standard electronics toolkit with
    various screwdrivers and plastic prying tools is necessary for disassembling
    the target device without causing damage.

### Software

  * **Logic Analyzer Software**:
      * **[Saleae
        Logic 2](https://www.saleae.com/pages/downloads?srsltid=AfmBOopqaRw53sSM7WrKtAesW3q-ThA7-aiYwGetgfsdcP3EGMTi5RsB)**:
        The recommended software for capturing and analyzing the bus
        communication. It is intuitive and has built-in protocol analyzers for
        SPI and LPC.
  * **BitLocker Decryption Tools**:
      * **[BitLocker SPI
        Toolkit](https://github.com/ReversecLabs/bitlocker-spi-toolkit)**: A
        collection of tools available on GitHub that can help in analyzing the
        captured data and extracting the VMK.

## Exploitation

### Reconnaissance and Target Identification

The first step is to identify the TPM chip on the target device's motherboard
and its communication bus. This requires a combination of physical inspection
and online research to minimize the risk of damaging client hardware.

1.  **Disassemble the Target Device**: Carefully disassemble the target laptop
    or computer to gain access to the motherboard. Take photos during this
    process to aid in reassembly. Once the motherboard is visible, identify its
    model number, which is usually printed directly on the PCB (e.g.,
    "`DA0X81MB6E0`"). This model number is crucial for the next step.

    ![Laptop Motherboard Model
    Number](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/bitlocker_attack_1.png)

2.  **Online Reconnaissance with Google Dorks**: Before probing or further
    disassembly, use the motherboard and laptop model numbers to find
    schematics, boardviews, and datasheets online. This can save a significant
    amount of time and prevent errors.

    **Effective Google Dorks:**

      * **For Schematics**:
          * `"[motherboard model]" schematic pdf`
          * `"[laptop model]" schematic`
          * `site:vinafix.com "[motherboard model]"`
          * `site:badcaps.net "[motherboard model]" schematic`
      * **For Datasheets (once a chip marking is found)**:
          * `"[chip marking]" datasheet pdf`
          * `filetype:pdf "[chip marking]" pinout`
      * **For Boardviews** (interactive layout files):
          * `"[motherboard model]" boardview`
          * `"[motherboard model]" filetype:brd`

    When reviewing these documents, search for keywords like **"TPM"**,
    **"SPI"**, and **"LPC"**. Schematics will show which pins on the TPM connect
    to which bus, and boardviews can help you locate the physical test points or
    traces on the PCB.

3.  **Locate the TPM or a Shared Bus Chip**: Use the information from your
    online research or by careful physical inspection to locate the target chip.
    TPM chips are often small, black components from manufacturers like
    Infineon, Nuvoton, or STMicroelectronics.

    **Warning:** It's important to remember that the more you disassemble a
    device, the higher the risk of causing accidental damage. If the TPM chip is
    not immediately visible on the side of the motherboard facing you, pause
    before attempting to remove the entire motherboard from the chassis. Instead
    of a full disassembly, investigate if other chips on the accessible side
    share the same communication bus as the TPM. For instance, the BIOS/UEFI is
    often stored on a separate flash memory chip that also communicates over an
    SPI bus. Since SPI is a shared bus, it's highly likely that the TPM traffic
    can be sniffed from the pins of this more accessible chip, such as the BIOS
    flash. This approach is significantly less invasive and reduces the risk of
    breaking delicate connectors or components.

    ![TPM Chip on a Motherboard](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/tpm_chip.png)

4.  **Identify the Communication Bus and Pinout**: Confirm the communication bus
    type (SPI or LPC) and the exact pinout using the datasheet for the
    identified chip. This information is critical for correctly connecting the
    logic analyzer in the next phase.

    ![Laptop Motherboard
    Datasheet](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/motherboard_datasheet.png)

    ![Shared SPI Bus](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/motherboard_datasheet_2.png)

    ![Flash Memory Chip
    Pinout](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/flash_memory_pinout.png)

    In the pictures above, you can see that the TPM chip and flash memory share
    the same SPI bus. In this example, the flash memory chip was much easier to
    get to, and there is an SPI pinout in the datasheet.

### Attaching the Logic Analyzer

With the TPM chip and its communication bus identified, the next step is to
connect a logic analyzer to intercept the data.

1.  **Prepare the Connection Points**: You will need to connect probes from your
    logic analyzer to the relevant pins on the TPM chip or a nearby header. In
    some cases, you may find an unpopulated debug header on the motherboard that
    provides easy access to these signals.

2.  **Connect the Logic Analyzer**: Use the SOIC8 clip/logic analyzer probes to
    make the connections or solder fine-gauge wires to the identified pins.
    Connect the other end of the wires or probes to your logic analyzer.

    ![Logic Analyzer Connection
    Probes](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/bitlocker_attack_2.png)

    ![Logic Analyzer Connection Probes
    Close-Up](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/bitlocker_attack_3.png)

### Data Capture and Key Extraction

This phase involves capturing the communication between the TPM and the CPU
during the boot process and then analyzing the captured data to find the
BitLocker VMK.

1.  **Configure the Logic Analyzer Software**: Use Logic Pro 2 or the software
    provided with your logic analyzer to configure the capture settings. You
    will need to set the sample rate high enough to accurately capture the
    signals (e.g., 100 MHz for a 33 MHz LPC bus).

2.  **Capture the Boot Process**: Start the capture on your logic analyzer and
    then power on the target device. Let the device boot into Windows. The logic
    analyzer will record all the data transmitted over the bus during this time.

    ![Logic Pro 2 Reading
    Signals](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/logic_analyzer_zoomed_in.png)

3.  **Decode the Captured Data**: Use a protocol decoder to make sense of the
    captured data. For SPI or LPC, you can use the built-in decoders in your
    logic analyzer software. There are also specialized decoders available for
    TPM communications.

4.  **Extract the Volume Master Key (VMK)**: The VMK is what you are looking for
    in the decoded data. It is a 256-bit key that is sent from the TPM to the
    CPU. You can often find it by searching for a specific header or pattern
    that precedes the key. The `bitlocker-spi-toolkit` on GitHub provides a
    Logic Pro 2 plugin and information on how to automatically identify and
    extract the VMK from the captured data.

    ![Decrypting the BitLocker
    VMK](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/bitlocker_attack_4.png)

### Decrypting the Drive

Once you have the VMK, you can use it to decrypt the BitLocker-protected drive.

1.  **Image the Target Drive**: It is highly recommended to create a bit-for-bit
    image of the target drive before attempting to decrypt it. This will prevent
    any accidental data loss.

2.  **Use `bitlocker-spi-toolkit` to Decrypt**: You can use the extracted VMK
    with `./mount-bitlocker` to mount the encrypted volume as a readable file
    system.

    ![Decrypting BitLocker
    drive](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/bitlocker_decrypt.png)

3.  **Access the Data**: Once the drive is mounted, you will have full access to
    all the files and data stored on it.

## Post-Exploitation

Once the encrypted drive is mounted and you have read/write access to the file
system, several post-exploitation techniques can be employed to establish
persistence, escalate privileges, or pivot to other systems in the network.
Since these modifications are made while the system is offline, they often
bypass security controls like Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) agents.

### Offline Credential Dumping

With access to the file system, you can retrieve the necessary registry hives to
dump local account credentials, including password hashes and the machine
account hash if the device is domain-joined.

1.  **Locate the Registry Hives**: Navigate to `C:\Windows\System32\config\`.

2.  **Copy the Hives**: Copy the `SAM`, `SECURITY`, and `SYSTEM` files to your
    analysis machine.

3.  **Dump Hashes with `secretsdump.py`**: Use Impacket's `secretsdump.py`
    script to perform an offline attack on the hives:

    ```bash
    secretsdump.py -sam SAM -system SYSTEM -security SECURITY LOCAL
    ```

    This command will dump the NTLM hashes for all local users. These hashes can
    then be used in pass-the-hash attacks or cracked offline. If the machine is
    part of a domain, the machine account hash (e.g., `LAPTOP-HOSTNAME$`) will
    also be dumped. This hash can be used for lateral movement within the
    domain.

### Establishing Persistence via Accessibility Features

A classic technique for gaining persistent access is to replace one of the
accessibility features available on the Windows lock screen with a command
prompt. This allows an attacker to launch a `SYSTEM`-level shell without needing
to log in.

1.  **Navigate to the System32 Directory**: Access the mounted Windows partition
    and go to the `C:\Windows\System32\` directory.
2.  **Backup the Original Utility**: Find `Utilman.exe` (the Ease of Access
    button) and rename it to `Utilman.exe.bak`. This creates a backup of the
    original file.
3.  **Replace with Command Prompt**: Make a copy of `cmd.exe` within the same
    directory and rename the copy to `Utilman.exe`.

After reassembling the laptop and booting it up, clicking the "Ease of Access"
icon on the Windows login screen will now launch a command prompt running with
`NT AUTHORITY\SYSTEM` privileges.

  ![Login Screen SYSTEM Command Prompt](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/bitlocker_system_escalate.png)

### Disabling Security Software

EDR and antivirus solutions are designed to prevent malicious activity on a
running system. By modifying their files while the system is offline, it's
possible to neutralize them before they can even start.

1.  **Locate Security Agent Directories**: Navigate to directories where EDR
    products may keep their files (e.g., `C:\Program Files\`, `C:\Program Files
    (x86)\`, `C:\Windows\System32\drivers\`).
2.  **Identify and Rename Directories**: Look for folders corresponding to
    security products installed on the system, such as:
      * `CrowdStrike`
      * `SentinelOne`
      * `Windows Defender` (Note: This is more complex as it's a core OS
        component)
      * `Carbon Black`
3.  **Rename the Folder**: Rename the main folder of the security product (e.g.,
    rename `SentinelOne` to `SentinelOne_Bak`).

When the system boots up again, the security agent's services will fail to find
their necessary files and will not start, leaving the system unprotected and
allowing you to execute further payloads without detection.

**Warning:** By renaming these files/folders, there is a chance that you may
cause the system to experience a Blue Screen of Death (BSOD) on boot. This is
particularly true when removing "boot first" drivers, such as SentinelOne and
Crowdstrike's files in `C:\Windows\System32\drivers\`. You will have to mess
with different combinations of what you do and do not rename to get the system
to boot again.

## Mitigation and Countermeasures

This attack exploits a weakness in the default, convenience-focused
configuration of BitLocker. While highly effective against such configurations,
it can be robustly mitigated by implementing additional layers of security. The
primary goal is to prevent the TPM from automatically releasing the Volume
Master Key (VMK) without user interaction.

### Primary Mitigation: Pre-Boot Authentication (PBA)

The single most effective countermeasure is to require Pre-Boot Authentication
(PBA). Instead of relying solely on the TPM, this method requires the user to
provide a secret before the Windows boot process begins. This secret can be a
PIN or a startup key stored on a USB drive. When PBA is enabled, the TPM will
not release the VMK until the correct secret has been provided, rendering the
bus sniffing attack ineffective as the key is never transmitted in a vulnerable
state.

#### How to Enable Pre-Boot Authentication via Group Policy

For domain-joined machines or systems with access to the Local Group Policy
Editor, this is the most reliable way to enforce PBA.

1.  **Open Group Policy Editor**: Press `Win + R`, type `gpedit.msc`, and press
    Enter.

2.  **Navigate to the BitLocker Policy**: Go to the following path: `Computer
    Configuration > Administrative Templates > Windows Components > BitLocker
    Drive Encryption > Operating System Drives`

3.  **Enable the "Require additional authentication at startup" Policy**:

      * Double-click this policy setting.
      * Set it to **Enabled**.
      * Under the "Options" pane, you can configure the specific protectors. For
        maximum security, uncheck "Allow BitLocker without a compatible TPM".
      * Configure TPM startup PIN to **"Require startup PIN with TPM"**.
      * *(Alternatively)* Configure TPM startup key to **"Require startup key
        with TPM"**.

    ![Group Policy Enforcing BitLocker PBA](assets/bitlocker-sniffing/bitlocker_gpo.png)

4.  **(Optional) Enable Enhanced PINs**: For stronger security, you can allow
    alphanumeric PINs instead of just numbers.

      * In the same Group Policy location, enable the **"Allow enhanced PINs for
        startup"** policy.

5.  **Apply the Policy**: Run `gpupdate /force` from an administrative command
    prompt to apply the policy immediately.

#### Adding the Protector with `manage-bde`

After the policy has been applied, the new protector must be added to the
existing BitLocker volume.

1.  **Open an Administrative Command Prompt or PowerShell**.

2.  **Add the TPM+PIN Protector**: Execute the following command:

    ```powershell
    manage-bde -protectors -add C: -TPMAndPIN
    ```

    You will be prompted to enter and confirm your desired PIN.

3.  **(Alternative) Add the TPM+Startup Key Protector**:

    ```powershell
    manage-bde -protectors -add C: -TPMAndStartupKey E:
    ```

    Replace `E:` with the drive letter of the USB drive where the startup key
    should be stored.

4.  **Verify the Protectors**: You can check that the new protector was added
    correctly by running:

    ```powershell
    manage-bde -status C:
    ```

    The output should now list "TPM and PIN" or "TPM and Startup Key" under the
    "Key Protectors" section.

### Secondary Mitigations and Defense-in-Depth

While PBA is the primary fix, other layers can increase the difficulty for an
attacker.

  * **Hardware Selection (fTPM)**: Modern CPUs from Intel (Platform Trust
    Technology - PTT) and AMD (fTPM) integrate TPM functionality directly into
    the CPU's firmware. Since there is no external chip or bus to sniff, these
    **Firmware-based TPMs (fTPMs)** are not vulnerable to this physical attack.
    When procuring new hardware, selecting systems with fTPMs is a significant
    security enhancement.
  * **UEFI/BIOS Password**: Setting a strong password in the UEFI/BIOS settings
    adds a crucial hurdle. It can prevent an attacker from booting from an
    external device, modifying the boot order, or changing hardware settings,
    all of which may be necessary to carry out other related attacks.
  * **Physical Security**: This attack requires sustained physical access to the
    device's internals. Implementing strong physical security controls, such as
    secure storage for laptops, access control to facilities, and tamper-evident
    seals, is a fundamental and effective deterrent.

### Factors Preventing Mitigation (Client Considerations)

Implementing these mitigations is not always straightforward, and clients may
have valid operational reasons for hesitating. It's important to understand and
address these concerns.

  * **User Friction and Help Desk Burden**: The most common objection to
    enabling a pre-boot PIN is the impact on user experience. Users must enter a
    PIN every time they reboot their machine, which can be seen as an
    inconvenience. This also leads to an increase in help desk tickets for
    forgotten PINs. If a PIN is forgotten, the only recourse is the 48-digit
    BitLocker Recovery Key, which can be cumbersome for support staff to provide
    and for users to enter.

  * **Remote Management and Automation**: PBA breaks many remote management
    workflows. If an administrator needs to reboot a fleet of machines remotely
    to apply patches, they cannot enter the BitLocker PIN. This means a machine
    will not fully boot until someone physically enters the PIN, disrupting
    maintenance windows and creating significant operational overhead. While
    solutions like Intel vPro or remote KVMs can sometimes help, they are not
    universally deployed.

  * **Deployment at Scale**: In environments without robust, centralized
    management tools like Microsoft Group Policy or Intune, enforcing these
    settings on every endpoint is a major challenge. Manually configuring each
    machine is not feasible for large organizations.

  * **Hardware Limitations**: The recommendation to use fTPM over dTPM is only
    practical during a hardware refresh cycle. Most organizations have a fleet
    of devices with varying hardware ages and cannot simply replace all
    vulnerable dTPM-based systems overnight due to budget constraints.
